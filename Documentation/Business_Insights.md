# Business Insights

Findings from the Sales & Customer Performance report (2022–2025, 300,000 transactions).

> **Reading guide.** All figures are for the full period with no filters unless a year is named. Every number can be reproduced in the report by clearing the slicers. The dataset is synthetic, so a few patterns (for example identical return rates across channels) are likely artefacts of data generation; they are listed in the last section rather than presented as business facts.

## 1. Growth is real but slowing

Sales grew from **$65.2M (2022)** to **$96.4M (2025)**, a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of **13.9%**. Year-over-year growth is decelerating every year: **+17.1% (2023), +13.4% (2024), +11.4% (2025)**.

**So what:** the business is healthy, but each year adds less growth than the last. Whatever drove 2023 is losing strength, which makes insights 2 and 3 important.

## 2. All net growth comes from Online

Online's share of sales rose from **40.7% to 63.6%** between 2022 and 2025. The other channels are shrinking in absolute terms, not just in share:

| Channel | 2024 YoY | 2025 YoY |
|---|---:|---:|
| Online | +32.9% | +26.0% |
| Retail stores | −3.2% | −6.9% |
| Partners | −6.5% | −8.2% |

Online growth is also slowing (+38.0% in 2023, +32.9% in 2024, +26.0% in 2025). The shift looks the same in every region (Online is 52–54% everywhere).

Channels are almost identical on profitability: margin 24.1–24.3%, average order value $1.07–1.09K, return rate about 6%. In this dataset the channel shift therefore changes where revenue comes from, not how profitable it is.

**So what:** total growth now depends on one channel whose own growth is decelerating while two other channels decline.

## 3. Growth comes from existing customers spending more, not from new customers

Active customers rose only from **16,616 (2022) to 18,181 (2025), +9.4%**, while sales rose 48%. Sales per active customer grew from **$3.9K to $5.3K (+35%)**. Customers making their first purchase in the dataset fell from **2,701 (2023) to 556 (2024) to 108 (2025)**.

**So what:** the customer base is almost fully acquired and growth is being squeezed from the same customers. That is sustainable only while spend per customer keeps rising.

## 4. One product drives almost a third of revenue at half the margin

**Laptop Model 190** generates **$101.1M, 31.1% of all sales** and 51.5% of Electronics sales. It sold in 30,144 orders; the median product has 56. The next-best product accounts for only 6.4% of the remaining sales.

Its margin is **14.4%**, against **28.6%** for the rest of the business. It brings in 31.1% of revenue but only **18.5% of profit**, and pulls company margin down from 28.6% to the reported **24.2%**.

This also explains the Electronics picture. Electronics shows a 21.5% margin, the lowest category, but **excluding this one product Electronics earns 29.0%**, as good as any other category.

**So what:** the report's category-level conclusions are really a single-product story. It is both the largest dependency risk (stock-outs, supplier terms, price changes) and the largest single margin lever: each +1 percentage point of margin on this product is worth about **$1.0M** of profit.

## 5. Deep discounts destroy margin and do not lift basket size

Profit margin falls steadily as the discount rises:

| Discount | Margin | Share of sales |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0% | 31.8% | 38.1% |
| 5% | 28.2% | 20.0% |
| 10% | 24.3% | 13.8% |
| 15% | 19.8% | 11.6% |
| 20% | 14.8% | 9.2% |
| 30% | 2.8% | 4.4% |
| 40–50% | **−23.4%** | 2.9% |

The **14,647 orders discounted by 40–50%** generated $9.5M in sales and a **loss of $2.2M**. In total, discounts gave away **$36.3M (10.1% of list-price value, equal to 46% of total profit)**.

There is no sign that discounts buy volume: average quantity per order line is flat at about 2.1 units across every discount level. This is observational data and cannot prove what demand would have been without a discount, but it gives no support to deep discounting.

**So what:** the 10.1% average discount looks harmless. The original subcategory scatter chart did show margin differences between subcategories (18.8% for Laptops to 31.2% for Team Sports), but it could not reveal the discount effect because every subcategory averages about 10%. The damage is in the tail.

## 6. Deep discounting is an Outlet-store problem

Discount depth is driven by store type, not by product category:

| Store type | Stores | Avg discount | Sales share | Profit share | Margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Standard | 121 | 5.3% | 64.0% | 74.1% | 28.0% |
| Flagship | 30 | 5.3% | 15.8% | 18.2% | 28.0% |
| Outlet | 49 | **25.0%** | 20.2% | **7.7%** | **9.2%** |

Outlets never sell below a 10% discount and go up to 50%, while Standard and Flagship stores stop at 30%. **All 14,647 orders discounted by 40–50% are Outlet orders**, and Outlets account for 97% of the loss on loss-making order lines ($2.78M of $2.86M). 40% of Outlet order lines carry a discount of 25% or more (about 1% elsewhere). Outlets also sell less per store ($1.34M vs $1.70–1.72M) and earn roughly a quarter of the profit per store ($124K vs about $480K). Margin is low in every channel inside Outlets (9.0–9.7%), so this is a store-format effect.

**So what:** one in five sales dollars goes through a format that returns less than 8% of profit.

## 7. North and East targets look disproportionately high

Against targets, South (188.8%) and Central (157.9%) are far above plan, while North (78.8%) and East (77.8%) together stayed below their combined target in every one of the 48 months.

But North and East have only **20 stores each**, against 60 in Central and South and 40 in West. **Sales per store is almost identical in every region ($1.60–1.67M)**. Targets are not: target per store is **$2.1M in North and East** against $1.0M in Central and $0.9M in South.

If the same $249.6M total target were allocated by store count, every region would land between **128% and 134%** of target.

Two further points:
- The company as a whole has never missed a monthly target (lowest month: 111.8%, June 2022), and annual target growth (8.7–11.5%) has stayed below actual growth (11.4–17.1%).
- Regional achievement differences are therefore at least partly explained by how the target was split. This dataset cannot show whether execution also differs between regions.

**So what:** the red bars on the Target vs Actual page suggest that target allocation should be reviewed before drawing conclusions about North and East execution. Operational data (staffing, local demand, store age) would be needed to rule execution issues out.

## 8. Returns are concentrated in Electronics

**6.0% of order lines** are returned, a rate that is flat over time (5.9–6.1%). By value that is **$21.7M, 6.7% of sales**. Net of refunds, sales are $302.9M, not $324.6M.

By category, Home & Kitchen (8.0%) and Electronics (7.9%) have the highest return rates, against about 4% for Furniture and Office Supplies. Because Electronics orders are large, it holds **70.7% of refunded value ($15.3M)**, and Laptop Model 190 alone accounts for **$7.7M (35%)**.

**So what:** the headline sales figure includes refunded orders. The return problem is a high-value Electronics problem more than a general one.

## 9. Customer revenue is moderately concentrated

The **top 20 customers account for only 6.7%** of sales, so the dashboard's Top 20 chart does not indicate dependence on individual customers. Concentration shows up further down the distribution: the **top 10% of customers generate 48.5%** of sales and the top 20% generate 63.1%.

By segment, Corporate is 20% of customers but **34.7% of sales**. A Corporate customer spends $28.2K on average, 2.3× a Consumer customer ($12.4K), with a larger average order ($2.0K vs $0.8K). Margins are identical across segments (about 24.2%).

**So what:** the risk is a broad group of high-value accounts, not a few named customers. Corporate is the segment where retention work pays off most per customer.

## 10. Strong, stable Q4 seasonality

**November (10.3%) and December (12.1%) together are 22.4% of annual sales**, against an average of 8.3% per month. A smaller summer bump appears in June–July (about 8.5% each). The pattern is nearly identical in all four years.

**So what:** inventory, staffing and promotions should be planned around Q4; it is also when the deep-discount problem (insight 5) costs the most in absolute terms.

---

## Data caveats

- The dataset is synthetic. Several patterns are suspiciously uniform and should not be read as real-world findings: identical return rates across channels, segments and store types; identical margins across channels and segments; near-identical sales per store across regions.
- The dominance of Laptop Model 190 is probably a generator artefact. It is treated here as a case study of how to detect and communicate product concentration.
- Customer names are not unique (320 names are shared by 20,000 customers, about 63 per name), so every customer figure here is calculated per `CustomerID`. A chart grouped by name alone would combine dozens of different customers.
- "New customers" means customers whose first purchase in the dataset falls in that year; the data has no separate acquisition record, and 2022 is the starting year.
- The refunded value is treated as lost sales. The data does not say whether returned goods were restocked, so no profit-after-returns figure is claimed.
- All discount/margin relationships are observational; no causal claim is made.
